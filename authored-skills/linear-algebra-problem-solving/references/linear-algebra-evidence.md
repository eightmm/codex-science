# Linear algebra evidence contract

Use this reference to distinguish algebraic structure from floating-point computation. A matrix is a representation relative to declared bases; numerical outputs inherit scaling, conditioning, and precision limits.

## Claim semantics

Assign one class to every stable claim ID and one status: `supported`, `contradicted`, `inconclusive`, or `unavailable`.

- **Proven result:** give the field, dimensions, spaces, basis, and exact assumptions; provide a derivation or theorem for claims about rank, nullity, existence, uniqueness, kernel/image, factorization, eigenstructure, or definiteness. Separate exact algebraic multiplicity from computed approximations.
- **Numerically verified result:** identify matrix and right-hand-side data, scaling, algorithm, precision, stopping rule, residual norm, backward error, and conditioning evidence. State what finite checks support the result. A small residual alone does not establish small forward error or exact rank/eigenstructure.
- **Simulated estimate:** reserve for estimates generated by a random matrix/data model or a stochastic algorithm; name the probability model, sampling unit, seed, sample size, estimator, and Monte Carlo uncertainty. Do not label deterministic rounding or a single randomized run a probability theorem.
- **Conjecture:** use for patterns or structural interpretations that lack an exact proof or adequate numerical evidence. State a falsifying case or next calculation; do not promote a conjecture from repeated floating-point observations alone.

Keep a proved identity and a floating-point approximation as separate claims when the latter relies on different assumptions.

## Source dependencies and independence

Record matrix/vector source, release or revision, dimensions, units/scaling, transformations, missingness, basis order, and whether entries are exact or rounded. Pin language, BLAS/LAPACK or other numerical-library version, algorithm, precision, tolerances, and hardware when it can affect the result. Record row/column permutations and scaling explicitly.

For a linear system, preserve both the original `A, b` and the exact transformed/scaled system. For a matrix representing a map, specify domain and codomain bases and coordinate ordering; a matrix copied without those bases is not a complete map specification. For a least-squares problem, record the norm/objective and whether weighting or regularization changes the target.

For eigenproblems, identify whether the matrix is symmetric/Hermitian, normal, generalized, sparse, or nonnormal, and whether the requested output is an eigenvalue, eigenspace, invariant subspace, or spectral statistic. For a quadratic form, retain the symmetry/Hermitian convention and field.

Two solver outputs are not independent confirmation if they share source data, matrix construction, preprocessing, factorization routines, thresholds, or libraries. Mark common dependencies and describe algorithmic agreement as a cross-check with that boundary. Preserve the original matrix and transformations so a reviewer can reconstruct the represented problem.

## Uncertainty, conditioning, and error

Separate uncertainty in input data, model/rounding, conditioning, algorithmic stability, iterative stopping, and randomization. Report residuals with norm and scale. Where applicable report backward error, condition estimate, and a forward-error or sensitivity interpretation; explain if a forward bound is unavailable. Small backward error can coexist with large forward error when the problem is ill-conditioned.

Numerical rank depends on a threshold and perturbation scale. Record the singular-value spectrum or equivalent evidence, threshold rule, and sensitivity to reasonable threshold/precision changes. Numerical eigenvalues of nonnormal or defective matrices can be sensitive; report residual, invariant-subspace evidence, and perturbation limitations rather than treating rounded eigenvectors as exact.

When exact arithmetic is infeasible, distinguish an estimate of a condition number from a certified bound. If the input itself is rounded or measured, include that perturbation scale when interpreting singular values and rank. State whether residuals are absolute or relative and avoid comparing values computed with different norms or scaling without conversion.

## Domain-specific controls

- Use exact rational or symbolic small cases with known rank, solution set, determinant, or spectrum to check implementation and conventions.
- Check dimensions, rank-nullity, orthogonality/unitarity, factorization reconstruction, symmetry/Hermitian structure, and positive-definiteness assumptions.
- For least squares, verify residual orthogonality to the column space and compare QR/SVD behavior when conditioning warrants; do not use normal equations as a control when they square the conditioning problem.
- For a known solution, construct a right-hand side and verify recovery under the declared scaling; use a near-singular companion case to expose sensitivity.
- Compare exact and floating-point results on a small case and check invariance under a consistent basis change.

Controls test conventions and implementation on declared cases; they do not establish performance for all dimensions or matrix classes.

For randomized low-rank or iterative methods, compare against a small dense/exact case where possible, fix and record seeds, and vary the sketch or starting vector. A single successful reconstruction can miss an unfavorable subspace or seed; describe the tested rank and spectral gap conditions.

## Sensitivity and fragility

Vary input perturbations at justified scales, precision, algorithm, scaling, stopping tolerance, rank threshold, and matrix size/structure. Predeclare the observable (solution, subspace, rank decision, eigenvalue, or other target) and its acceptable change. Treat a result as fragile when the classification, selected solution, numerical rank, relevant spectral subspace, or reported digits change beyond that bound. Report the perturbation model and do not select a threshold after observing the desired classification.

When comparing algorithms, use the same input and target, report the differing numerical pathway, and note common libraries or preprocessing. Separate sensitivity to the mathematical problem from instability of a particular algorithm.

For approximate subspaces, compare principal angles or an explicitly defined subspace distance rather than elementwise eigenvector differences, which can change under sign or basis rotation. For a computed least-squares solution, perturb both `A` and `b` only within a declared input-error model and report changes in fitted values as well as coefficient vectors when those answer different questions.

## Applicability and failure detection

Limit conclusions to the stated field, dimensions, basis, input precision, matrix structure, conditioning range, scaling, algorithm, and tested perturbations. A successful factorization or residual check does not establish a property outside the recorded matrix and tolerance range.

State whether conclusions concern one supplied matrix or a family with stated structure and bounds. Do not generalize a tolerance or rank threshold calibrated on one scale to matrices with different units or magnitude without an explicit rescaling argument.

For matrices with uncertain entries, distinguish the computed matrix result from the claim about the unknown underlying matrix.

| Failure mode | Detection and response |
|---|---|
| Dimension or basis mismatch | Check every map/product and reconstruction under declared coordinates; stop symbolic manipulation until consistent. |
| Ill-conditioning hidden by a small residual | Estimate conditioning and assess forward sensitivity; report backward and forward evidence separately. |
| Numerical rank mistaken for exact rank | Vary precision and prespecified threshold; report ambiguity or an exact proof if available. |
| Unstable factorization or loss of structure | Check reconstruction, orthogonality, symmetry, and structure-preserving invariants; change method or qualify. |
| Normal equations amplify conditioning | Compare with QR/SVD or document why the condition permits them; do not call residual size alone validation. |
| Eigenvalue/eigenvector sensitivity | Check residuals and invariant subspaces under perturbation; avoid exact multiplicity claims from approximate output. |
| Scaling or permutation omitted | Reconstruct in original coordinates and retain transforms; invalidate results that cannot be mapped back. |
| Randomized algorithm variability | Repeat under recorded independent seeds and report Monte Carlo or algorithmic variation; do not present one run as deterministic proof. |

Keep failed computations and ambiguous numerical classifications. Never silently replace an ill-conditioned or rank-deficient input with a nearby convenient matrix.

## Sources

- Jim Hefferon (2020), *Linear Algebra*, fourth edition: vector spaces, maps, systems, eigenstructure, and proof-oriented reasoning.
- Nicholas J. Higham (2002), *Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms*, second edition: conditioning, backward error, and floating-point stability.
- James H. Wilkinson (1965), *The Algebraic Eigenvalue Problem*: numerical eigenvalue computation and its sensitivity.

These works inform the mathematical and numerical distinctions; they do not validate a particular input matrix or implementation.
